## Tier 1 — Highest leverage (do these no matter what)

**1. Reframe the CLL vs AUL cross-architecture comparison (within-architecture framing).**

This is your single biggest unforced error and it costs nothing to fix. Right now §5.2 says "encoders > causals" by directly comparing R values built from two different base metrics. A reviewer will hammer this. Rewrite the section so the headline becomes two parallel within-architecture findings instead of one cross-architecture finding:

- "Among the five encoder models, three show grand R > 1, with values up to 3.88; the remaining two are below 1."
- "Among the five causal models, none shows grand R > 1; values range from 0.05 to 0.81."
- "Direct numerical comparison of R across architectures is avoided because CLL and AUL are not calibrated to a common scale (see §S9.4); the qualitative split — encoder models contain the only R > 1 grand cases — is robust to this caveat because it depends only on the within-architecture sign of (R − 1)."

Then update the abstract, intro contributions list, and conclusion to mirror this language. The finding is preserved; the methodological objection is defused. This is a 2-hour rewrite.

## Tier 2 — Strong leverage (do if you have time)

**5. Rewrite the abstract to lead with the actionable finding, not the architectural one.**

Practitioners and editors care about the decision tree. Rewrite the abstract so the first sentence is the safety-budget implication, not the encoder/causal split. Something like:

> "Debiasing pipelines for large language models typically assume that removing a bias requires comparable computational effort to introducing one. We test this assumption across 10 multilingual models, 3 languages, 11 bias categories, and 6 debiasing methods, and find it fails systematically — but only under specific conditions. Weight-editing methods such as BiasEdit can require up to 16× more gradient steps to remove a bias than to inject it, while inference-time methods such as Self-Debias bypass the asymmetry entirely (R = 0). The effect concentrates in encoder models (3 of 5 show grand R > 1; no causal model does) and in bias categories well-represented in pretraining data, while India-specific categories (caste, religion) and gender show near-symmetric dynamics. We translate these findings into a practitioner decision tree for allocating debiasing compute budgets…"

Same data, different ordering, much stronger pull for an editor skimming submissions.

**6. Add a 1-paragraph "Threats to Validity" subsection at the end of §5 (before §6).**

You already have S9 in the supplement, but reviewers don't always read supplements before drafting their review. Lift the four most important caveats — CLL/AUL confound, R = 4.0 ceiling, n < 30 categories, LoRA-only — into a single visible paragraph in the main paper. This pre-empts the most common reviewer objections in the place reviewers will actually see them. Reviewers who feel pre-empted write softer reviews; reviewers who feel they "caught" something write harsh ones. Make them feel pre-empted.

**7. Soften the mechanistic claims in §5.6.**

Right now §5.6 says biased states "occupy flatter loss landscape regions" and presents the Hessian story as a "preliminary geometric perspective." Cite Dinh et al. 2017 (sharp minima can generalize) as a counterpoint, and explicitly downgrade the section to "exploratory." One paragraph of intellectual humility here costs you nothing and removes a target. The section should end with: "We present this as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than a confirmed mechanism; rigorous mechanistic claims would require [Hessian analysis on all 10 models / multiple seeds / multiple evaluation batches], which we leave to future work."

**8. Strengthen the practitioner framing in §6.**

Add a small table that translates the findings into specific compute-budget multipliers, e.g.:

| If you are debiasing... | Using method... | Allocate at least |
|---|---|---|
| Encoder model (BERT-family) | BiasEdit | 16× injection budget |
| Encoder model | INLP/contrastive | 4× injection budget |
| Causal model | DAMA | 4× injection budget |
| Causal model | LoRA fine-tuning | 1× injection budget |
| Any model | Self-Debias | No additional weight-update budget |

This kind of concrete deliverable is what gets a paper recommended for acceptance even when reviewers have methodological quibbles, because the editor can point to it as "useful to the community." It also gives the paper a citable artifact beyond the prose.

**9. Reframe the n < 30 categories as honestly excluded, not "interpreted with caution".**

Right now you treat physical-appearance, sexual-orientation, age, and disability as included-with-caveats. The bootstrap CIs in S7 span from below 1 to above 1 for all four, which means you cannot conclude anything about the direction of R for these categories. Move them out of the headline category-level analysis (Table 6 and §5.4) and into a clearly labeled "categories excluded for insufficient power" footnote, with the bootstrap analysis in S7 as justification. This *strengthens* your remaining category claims because the surviving categories (gender, race, race-color, nationality, socioeconomic, caste, religion) all have adequate n. A reviewer who sees you proactively excluding underpowered categories will trust you more, not less.

## Tier 3 — Polish (worth doing if you have a free afternoon)

**10. Add 1-2 sentences justifying θ = 0.7 as the primary threshold.** Right now it appears as a design choice without rationale. Something like: "θ = 0.7 was chosen because it represents a strong stereotypical preference (the model is roughly 70% confident in the stereotypical completion) while remaining reachable by all 10 tested models within the injection budget — at θ = 0.8, several causal models never crossed the threshold, making R undefined." This is a one-line defense that prevents a reviewer from calling θ arbitrary.

**11. Add seed-level variance reporting for the headline R values.** You report 95% CIs across 3 seeds in S2 and S6 but the main-paper Tables 4 and 5 show point estimates only. Add ± CI to at least the encoder rows in Table 4 — it makes the variance visible and shows you're not hiding it. Cost: zero new computation; you have the numbers already in S2.

Most authors waste this opportunity. Don't.

One footnote in the introduction defining it as "asymmetry between a forward and reverse process such that the reverse requires more effort or follows a different path" eliminates a small comprehension friction.
